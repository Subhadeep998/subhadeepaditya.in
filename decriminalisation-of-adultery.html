Decriminalisation of Adultery: Upholding Equality, Privacy, and Individual Autonomy

Subhadeep AdityaLL.B. student · Techno India University, Kolkata

The judgment of the Supreme Court of India in Joseph Shine v. Union of India marked a transformative moment in Indian constitutional jurisprudence. On 27 September 2018, a Constitution Bench unanimously declared Section 497 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860, unconstitutional, thereby decriminalising adultery. While adultery continues to be a valid ground for divorce and other civil matrimonial remedies, the Court held that it should not attract criminal liability. The decision reaffirmed the constitutional principles of equality, dignity, privacy, and personal liberty, reflecting the evolving understanding of individual rights under the Constitution of India.

Section 497 criminalised adultery by punishing a man who engaged in a consensual sexual relationship with a married woman without the consent or connivance of her husband. Notably, the provision treated the married woman as a victim rather than an equal participant in the act and denied her any criminal liability. Moreover, only the husband of the woman could initiate prosecution, while the wife of the adulterous man had no corresponding legal remedy. This framework reflected an outdated patriarchal notion that regarded women as the property of their husbands rather than as autonomous individuals with independent legal identities.

The Supreme Court found this classification to be manifestly arbitrary and violative of Article 14 of the Constitution, which guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the laws. By creating unequal rights and obligations solely on the basis of gender, Section 497 failed to satisfy the constitutional requirement of reasonable classification. The Court observed that the Constitution does not permit laws that reinforce stereotypes or institutionalise gender discrimination.

The judgment also strengthened the constitutional guarantee of personal liberty under Article 21. The Court recognised that decisions concerning intimate personal relationships fall within the sphere of individual autonomy and privacy. Criminal law, the Court reasoned, should not regulate consensual relationships between adults where no public harm or threat to society is involved. Although adultery may amount to a moral or matrimonial wrong, it does not constitute a criminal offence warranting state punishment.

An equally significant aspect of the judgment was its recognition of the dignity of women. The Court rejected the assumption that a married woman lacks agency or independent decision-making capacity. Instead, it affirmed that women possess equal constitutional status and cannot be treated as passive subjects under the authority of their husbands. This reasoning aligned with the constitutional vision of substantive equality and reinforced India's commitment to eliminating discriminatory legal provisions rooted in historical social norms.

The decision was also influenced by the Supreme Court's earlier recognition of the fundamental right to privacy in the landmark judgment of Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India. Building upon that precedent, the Court held that the State should not intrude into the private sphere of consenting adults unless there exists a compelling constitutional justification. Personal choices concerning marriage and intimate relationships, though subject to civil consequences, remain protected aspects of individual liberty.

Importantly, the Court clarified that decriminalising adultery does not legitimise or encourage marital infidelity. Adultery continues to constitute a valid ground for divorce, judicial separation, and other matrimonial proceedings under personal laws. The judgment merely removed the criminal sanction, recognising that criminal law should be reserved for conduct that threatens public order, safety, or the rights of others.

In conclusion, the decision in Joseph Shine v. Union of India represents a significant advancement in Indian constitutional law. By striking down Section 497, the Supreme Court reinforced the principles of equality, dignity, privacy, and personal autonomy while eliminating a provision that perpetuated gender inequality. The judgment reflects the dynamic nature of constitutional interpretation and reaffirms that individual liberty and equal treatment under the law are foundational values of a democratic society governed by the Constitution.
